# How Bella Phelps Helped Make Madeira Embroidery Famous

Elizabeth "Bella" Phelps was born in Funchal in **1820**, the eldest daughter of a British wine-trading family that had lived on the island since 1784. She spent part of her life on something that outlasted the family business. It was a local embroidery craft that reached London and remains one of Madeira's best-known exports today.

Phelps did not invent the island's embroidery tradition, though women in Madeira had already decorated **household linen** for generations. After watching local women and girls at work, she opened an embroidery school of her own in Funchal. She improved their patterns and sent the results to London, where they found an international audience.

## Her Family Had Settled in Funchal in 1784

Phelps's grandparents, **William and Elizabeth Phelps**, left Gloucestershire for Madeira in January 1784 and settled on Rua do Carmo in Funchal. Their son Joseph later ran the family firm, Phelps Page & Co, which exported Madeira wine to ports from New York to Amsterdam.

Joseph married Elizabeth Dickinson in 1819, and the couple went on to have **eleven children**, all born in Funchal. Bella was their eldest daughter.

## Her Mother Led a Girls' School for Over 40 Years

Joseph and his wife Elizabeth were also committed to education. Joseph opened a boys' school based on the Lancasterian method. Elizabeth ran a similar school for girls, teaching reading, writing and arithmetic, plus **sewing and embroidery**.

According to the Madeira historian Cristina Trindade, Elizabeth stayed in charge of the girls' school for **more than 40 years**. It was in that same household, and against that same backdrop of female education, that Bella grew interested in the island's embroidery.

## A Funchal Exhibition in 1850 Showed Her First Embroidery

After watching the skill of local women and girls, Bella decided to open an **embroidery school** in Funchal. She improved the patterns, modernized the methods, and imported linen and thread from England.

She then sent finished embroidery sets to London. She had help from her sister, known in the family as Kitty, and from Lady Marian Alford, who had contacts in London's needlework circles. The results were shown at a local exhibition of Madeiran products held in **1850** at the Palácio de São Lourenço in Funchal.

## The Great Exhibition of 1851 Made It Known in London

The work was so well received in Funchal that Bella put it forward for the **Great Exhibition of 1851**. It was held at the Crystal Palace in London, in the Portuguese section, shown alongside displays shared with Iberian and colonial exhibits.

Visitors praised the embroidery's **delicacy and quality**. That exposure was the point at which Madeira's embroidery crossed from a local craft into an internationally recognized export good, soon appearing in Victorian trousseaus.

## Tens of Thousands of Women Embroidered From Home

The exhibition opened a market in Britain and Europe. Production grew into a **putting-out system**. Women received cut cloth with the design already stamped on it, embroidered it at home, and returned the finished pieces through agents or firms.

At its height, the trade employed **tens of thousands** of women across Madeira. For many of them, it was one of the only ways to earn money of their own. The craft became known as *bordado da Madeira*, and the women who made it, the *bordadeiras*.

## A 1935 Law Followed a Decade of Falling Prices

The industry was not without trouble. The historian Benedita Câmara has traced a period of market failure **between 1924 and 1934**. The price, demand and reputation of Madeira embroidery all fell.

In **1935**, the Portuguese government introduced quality regulation for the trade, enforced through the intermediary action of a guild. Established firms accepted the rules. It let them share a collective reputation built on quality, rather than compete on price alone.

## She Died in 1893, Decades After Leaving Madeira

Bella Phelps later suffered a **severe leg injury** in a horse-riding accident. She moved to Clapham Common in London in 1860 and lived there until her death in **1893**.

She never returned to run the school she had founded in Funchal. But by then the trade she had helped start had a **life of its own** on the island she left behind.

## About 1,000 Embroiderers Still Work on the Island Today

Madeira embroidery survives, though at a much smaller scale than in its peak years. Tourism officials estimate about a dozen companies and roughly **1,000 embroiderers** now work in the craft daily.

Every genuine piece carries a **numbered seal** from IVBAM. IVBAM is the regional institute that merged Madeira's old wine and embroidery authorities in 2006. The seal separates hand-worked originals from machine copies.

Visitors can see historic pieces at the Núcleo Museológico do Bordado da Madeira in Funchal. The **Bordal workshop** also displays the craft Bella Phelps helped start.
