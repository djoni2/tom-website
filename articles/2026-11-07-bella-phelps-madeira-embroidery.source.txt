Os primeiros Phelps a virem para a Madeira foram o casal William (1749-1831) e Elisabeth Phelps (1957-1829), dando origem a uma família numerosa e próspera, sobretudo nos negócios do vinho Madeira. Dos sete filhos que tiveram, mandaram estudar para Londres os rapazes, como era habitual entre os britânicos residentes na Madeira. Um desses rapazes, Joseph, veio a conhecer, quando estudava, a mulher com quem haveria de casar, também ela Elisabeth, nascida Dickinson. Joseph e Elizabeth Phelps contraíram matrimónio em agosto de 1819, instalando-se na Madeira, na casa da família, no atual n.º 6 da rua do Carmo.
Enquanto Joseph se embrenhou de imediato nos negócios da família, Elisabeth foi tendo filhos, num total de onze, passando os dias ocupada com a educação das crianças, a gestão da casa e do pequeno hotel que ali tinham instalado, sempre bem assessorada por um exército de criados, ao mesmo tempo que mantinha uma intensa vida social, fazendo e recebendo visitas, dinamizando serões e participando em diversas atividades de beneficência do Funchal, como o Asilo de Mendicidade e a Misericórdia do Funchal.
A família possuía uma propriedade no Monte, a Quinta do Prazer - atual Monte Palace - usada sobretudo para veraneio, na qual promoviam convívios e diversões com algumas das mais importantes famílias madeirenses da época, como os Blandy, os Stoddart, os Gordon e os Oliveiras.
Apesar das vidas atarefadas, o casal Phelps comprometera-se profundamente com o desenvolvimento e melhoramento das condições de vida dos madeirenses, em particular os mais pobres, levando a cabo, para além das já referidas atividades benemerentes, uma obra de grande relevo no campo da educação: a escola Lancastriana para rapazes e raparigas, dirigidas respetivamente por Joseph e Elizabeth. A escola Lancasteriana aplicava o método criado por Joseph Lancaster em Inglaterra, consistindo, de forma simplificada, em ensinar um conjunto de alunos, os quais depois se dedicavam a educar os mais jovens, e assim sucessivamente, vindo a permitir o acesso a um nível de conhecimentos até então impensável, sobretudo para as raparigas no Funchal.
A escola feminina, cujos estatutos chegaram aos nossos dias, implicava o cumprimento de determinadas regras: as meninas tinham de vir limpas, com o cabelo cortado e ser pontuais. O período de estudo decorria entre as 9 e as 12 horas da manhã, e das 14h às 17h da tarde, com um currículo composto por atividades que para além dos inevitáveis ler, escrever e contar, englobando também as artes da costura e do bordado. Para financiar a instituição obtiveram contributos de outras figuras importantes da comunidade estrangeira residente ou estante no Funchal, como a rainha Adelaide, os duques de Saxe-Weimar e o Duque de Leuchtenberg, o qual ofereceu uma biblioteca de 100 volumes. Elisabeth Phelps manteve-se à frente da escola por mais de 40 anos, sendo depois substituída por uma comissão que incluia o bispo e a condessa do Farrobo. Fora da gestão de Elizabeth Phelps, a escola acabaria, no entanto, por encerrar, devido ao agravamento dos problemas financeiros.
A filha mais velha de Elisabeth Phelps, também ela Elisabeth, a quem chamavam Bella, provavelmente para a distinguir da mãe, viria a ser de grande relevância para a Madeira, na medida em que se interessou pelo bordado que então se fazia na Madeira, decidindo abrir uma escola com o objetivo de cativar as jovens para a sua prática.
Pela correspondência familiar, sabemos que Bella mandou para Londres amostras de bordado, ao cuidado de uma irmã que ali residia e tinha contactos com a Kensington School of Needlework, de quem possivelmente recebeu alguma acessoria. O resultado desta iniciativa foi depois apresentado na exposição de produtos madeirenses realizada em 1850 no Palácio de São Lourenço, sendo os bordados tão bem recebidos que Bella resolveu mostrá-los na Grande Exposição Mundial de Londres de 1851, marcando o momento a partir do qual o bordado madeirense viria a alcançar os mercados estrangeiros, tornando-se uma das imagens de marca da ilha.
A irmã de Bella, Mary, logo depois em idade, legou também ela à Madeira um testemunho raro e precioso que permite conhecer o quotidiano de uma família inglesa no Funchal, dos hábitos da comunidade britânica do tempo, do contraste que faziam com as práticas da sociedade local, bem como a aspetos dessa vida social, como os bailes, concertos, excursões, passeios, festas e corridas.
O documento que contém toda esta informação é o Diário de Mary Phelps, iniciado em 1839 e estendendo-se até 1843, dando acesso não só a toda a informação já referida, como às atividades quotidianas da autora que, aos 17 anos, idade com que fez o primeiro registo, se via encarregada de tomar conta dos irmãos mais novos, nas ausências da mãe para Londres, servindo-lhes inclusivamente de professora de música. Pelo mesmo diário se observa igualmente que a educação das crianças Phelps incluía, ainda, o francês e o alemão, sendo o pai o responsável pela aprendizagem do português.
Mary refere-se também à beleza natural da Madeira, que a deslumbra, não se eximindo, no entanto, de criticar algumas ruas do Funchal, mal calcetadas e sujas, ou a falta de variedade de produtos no comércio local, o que, registe-se, abre uma janela inesperada e colorida para a vivência inglesa na Madeira e para a forma como se relacionava com a sociedade local, da qual também descreve os hábitos, o que lhe confere uma importância singular, na medida em que é um testemunho único para a análise da Madeira do seu tempo.
Muito agradeço à Dra Cláudia Faria, que se tem dedicado ao estudo desta família, e de cujo conhecimento me subsidiei.

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
British philanthropist (1820–1893)
Elizabeth "Bella" Phelps (1820 – 1893) was a British philanthropist, educator, and promoter of Madeira embroidery , best known for developing and commercializing the craft on the Portuguese island of Madeira.
Through her efforts, she transformed it into a thriving cottage industry that empowered local women economically. Her promotion of the embroidery led to its international recognition, particularly through its display at the Great Exhibition of 1851 in London, where it gained widespread acclaim and established itself as a luxury export item.
Early life
[ edit ]
Bella Phelps was born in Funchal , Madeira , the daughter of Joseph Phelps, a wine shipper and the co-owner of Phelps, Page & Co, and his wife, Elizabeth. [ 1 ] She was one of 11 children. [ 2 ] The Phelps family had been in Funchal since 1784. [ 2 ]
Influence on the Great Exhibition
[ edit ]
Phelps's promotional work culminated in the display of Madeira embroidery at the Great Exhibition in 1851, held at the Crystal Palace in London. Although Phelps did not attend or exhibit personally, the samples she had facilitated through her networks were showcased in the Portuguese section (often shared with Iberian or colonial displays). The embroidery, noted for its exceptional delicacy, purity, quality, and artistry, received tremendous acclaim from visitors, including British aristocracy and commercial interests familiar with Madeira through the wine trade and tourism. This exposure at the Great Exhibition marked a turning point, elevating Madeira embroidery from a local craft to an internationally recognized luxury good. It became a staple in Victorian ladies’ trousseaux and boosted exports, particularly to Britain and Europe. The success directly stemmed from Phelps’s earlier initiatives, including a local exhibition in Funchal in 1850 at the Palácio de São Lourenço , which generated interest leading to the London invitation.
Personal life
[ edit ]
Phelps suffered from a severe leg injury while horse riding. [ 1 ] She moved to Clapham Common in 1860 and died in 1893. [ 1 ]

Madeira has a water problem and a famous answer to it. Cloud piles up against the northern mountains and drops rain there almost daily, while the southern slopes where people actually wanted to live and farm stay dry for months. So the island was cut open. More than 2,000 kilometres of narrow stone channels, the levadas, were carved along contour lines and driven through the rock in tunnels, moving water from the wet side to the dry side by gravity alone, at grades so fine that a channel can run for kilometres and fall only a metre ( Levada ).
That network is the island's great inherited object, and it should be said plainly who built it. The earliest levadas were dug by enslaved people, Guanches taken from the Canary Islands and Africans brought through the Atlantic trade, alongside convicts shipped out from mainland Portugal , and later by hired men lowered on ropes over cliffs to cut ledges into basalt. The sugar wealth those channels irrigated rested on the same labour. What follows is a guide to Madeira's landscapes, museums and archives, and to the ways Madeirans remember their dead, including the more than forty people killed by the water in a single morning in 2010.
National Overview
Madeira is an autonomous region of Portugal about 800 kilometres southwest of Lisbon and rather closer to the Moroccan coast, made up of the main island of Madeira at 740.7 square kilometres, Porto Santo at 42.5, and the uninhabited Desertas and Selvagens groups, with a total population of 250,769 at the 2021 census ( Wikipedia ). The main island is the eroded top of a volcano: a spine of peaks rising to 1,862 metres at Pico Ruivo, cliffs that fall straight into deep water, and almost no flat ground, so the farmland is cut into terraces called poios and the settlements string along the coast.
Roughly a fifth of the island is covered by laurisilva, a laurel forest of a kind that once ran across southern Europe and now survives in quantity only here and on a few neighbouring archipelagos. It carries World Heritage listing, and the region's protected areas cover a large share of the land. Autonomy came with the constitutional settlement of 1976, and the regional government, assembly and courts all sit in Funchal, which holds close to half the population of the archipelago.
Notable Cities
Funchal. The capital fills an amphitheatre of terraced slopes above a wide bay, and its old cathedral, the Sé, was finished around 1514 under Manuel I, with a ceiling of Madeiran cedar inlaid in geometric patterns of ivory that's among the finest Manueline interiors anywhere ( Britannica ). Below it lies the Zona Velha, the old quarter of narrow streets where an art project has turned scores of battered doors into painted panels, and the Mercado dos Lavradores , the 1940 market building by Edmundo Tavares, where flower sellers in regional dress work the ground floor and fishmongers split black scabbardfish downstairs. Above the town, Monte holds the church of Nossa Senhora do Monte, the pilgrimage site that also contains the tomb of Charles I, last emperor of Austria, who died in exile on the island in 1922.
Funchal grew on sugar worked by enslaved labour before the industry moved to Brazil, and the wine trade that replaced it is why Madeira has cellars holding bottles from the eighteenth century. Photograph by PESP, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.
Câmara de Lobos. A working fishing town west of Funchal, its harbour packed with painted boats and its slopes carrying some of the oldest terraced vineyards on the island. Winston Churchill painted the bay in 1950, and the spot he set his easel is still marked.
Machico. The first landfall of the Portuguese and the seat of the island's second captaincy, Machico keeps a fifteenth-century parish church and the chapel of the Senhor dos Milagres, whose October festival is the largest religious gathering in the east of the island.
Santana and Vila Baleira. Santana on the north coast preserves the palheiros, small A-framed houses with steep thatched roofs painted in red, white and blue; Vila Baleira, the only town on Porto Santo, keeps the house where Christopher Columbus is said to have lived while married to the daughter of the island's first captain.
Historical Foundations
Portuguese captains under Henry the Navigator reached Porto Santo in 1418 and Madeira the following year, and settlement began around 1420 under João Gonçalves Zarco, Tristão Vaz Teixeira and Bartolomeu Perestrelo, who divided the islands into captaincies ( Madeira ). Nobody lived there before them. The settlers burned the forest to clear ground, a fire that later chroniclers claimed burned for years, and the ash made the first crops spectacular.
Sugar followed, brought from Sicily and the Mediterranean and planted on the irrigated southern terraces, and within two generations Madeira was one of the largest sugar producers in the western world: by 1480 Antwerp had some seventy ships working the Madeira sugar trade. That economy needed both water and hands, and it took both by force. Enslaved Guanches from the Canaries, enslaved Africans and Portuguese convicts cut the levadas, worked the mills and cut the cane, and the sugar fortunes of fifteenth and sixteenth century Funchal, the churches they endowed and the merchant houses they built, were paid for out of that labour. When Brazilian and Caribbean plantations undercut the island in the seventeenth century, sugar collapsed and vines took its place.
Wine made Madeira's second fortune and its international name. English merchants settled in Funchal in numbers after the Anglo-Portuguese commercial treaties, founding houses whose names still hang over the lodges, and the island's position on the westbound Atlantic route made it the last provisioning stop before America. Britain occupied the island twice, in 1801 and 1807, to keep it out of French hands. Emigration began in earnest in the nineteenth century and never really stopped, and in 1976 the archipelago became an autonomous region with its own government and assembly.
Linguistic and Etymological Roots
The island's name is simply the Portuguese word for wood, madeira, given for the forest that covered it when the first ships arrived. Porto Santo, the holy port, was named by crews who sheltered there from a storm. Funchal takes its name from funcho, fennel, which grew thickly on the slopes above the bay; Câmara de Lobos means the chamber of the wolves, after the monk seals that hauled out in its cove and have since become one of the rarest mammals in Europe; Machico is traditionally derived from Machim, the shipwrecked Englishman of a legend the settlers found convenient.
Madeiran Portuguese is instantly recognisable to other Portuguese speakers, mainly for what it does to vowels. Stressed vowels are drawn out and shifted, so that a word like pai comes out closer to poi, and the language has a lexicon of its own: semilha for potato where the mainland says batata, and a stock of terms from the wine and embroidery trades that came in from English. Emigration added further layers. Families returning from Venezuela brought Spanish turns of phrase and vocabulary into everyday Funchal speech, to the point that the mixture has its own half-joking name, and the Madeiran communities in Jersey, Johannesburg and Caracas each speak a Portuguese marked by where they went.
Cultural Identity
The levadas are the thing Madeirans point to first, and they remain a working irrigation system rather than a heritage exhibit. Water rights are ancient, minutely divided and still administered: a smallholder may be entitled to a channel's flow for a set number of minutes on a set day, and the levadeiro who walks the line to clear leaves and open sluices is a real job. Because every channel needs a maintenance path beside it, the network doubles as several hundred kilometres of walking route through country that would otherwise be unreachable.
Two crafts built a great deal of household income. Madeira embroidery grew from a domestic tradition into an export industry after Elizabeth Phelps, the Funchal-born daughter of an English wine shipper, organised a local exhibition in 1850 and got island work into the Portuguese section of the Great Exhibition in London the following year, where it was a hit ( Bella Phelps ). At its height the trade employed tens of thousands of women working at home, taking in cut cloth and marked patterns and returning finished pieces through agents, and it remains one of the few industries in which the island's rural women earned money in their own right ( Visit Madeira ). The village of Camacha did the same with willow, weaving furniture and baskets that were shipped across Europe.
Everyday food is distinct from mainland cooking. Beef is cut into cubes, threaded onto a bay laurel skewer and grilled as espetada; bread is baked as bolo do caco on a hot stone and served split and dripping with garlic butter; the drink is poncha, sugarcane spirit beaten with honey and lemon juice using a grooved wooden stick. The island also gave the world a musical instrument by accident: Madeiran emigrants who sailed for Hawaii in 1879 carried the small four-string machete with them, and the cabinetmakers among them began building it there under a new name, the ukulele.
Religion and Spirituality
Madeira is strongly Catholic, and its patron is Nossa Senhora do Monte, whose church stands at the top of a long flight of steps above Funchal. On 15 August pilgrims climb the last stretch on their knees, and the church holds both the small fifteenth-century statue of the Virgin and the tomb of the exiled Austrian emperor. In Machico the Senhor dos Milagres is honoured on 8 and 9 October, in memory of a flood that swept the crucifix out to sea and of its recovery afterwards, and the night procession draws people back from across the island.
The Christmas season, known simply as the Festa, runs longer here than almost anywhere in Portugal. It opens on 8 December when Funchal switches on its lights, which turn the whole amphitheatre of the bay into a lit slope for a month. Between 16 and 24 December come the missas do parto, the masses of the birth, held at dawn in parish churches with drums, brass and the local ukulele-sized instruments, after which the congregation spills into the street for bolo do caco, poncha and singing at six in the morning. Houses build a lapinha, a tiered nativity with wheat sprouted in dishes weeks in advance, the fireworks over the bay on New Year's Eve are among the largest displays anywhere, and the season closes on 15 January with Santo Amaro, when neighbours go from house to house eating the last of the Christmas food and taking down the decorations.
Remembrance and Mourning Traditions
Madeiran mourning follows the Portuguese pattern of the wake, the funeral mass, the seventh-day mass and the anniversary, with burial in family vaults, the jazigos, that hold several generations under one name. The great collective day is 1 and 2 November, All Saints and All Souls, when families clean the vaults, replace the flowers and stand together at the grave; in a place where the young have emigrated for two centuries, those dates are also when the returning children of the household are most likely to be present. At roadsides and above the steep bends of mountain roads stand alminhas , niches with an image of souls in purgatory where passers-by leave a coin or a flower for the dead, including for people whose graves are in Caracas or Johannesburg.
The island's sharpest modern grief has a date. On 20 February 2010 an intense cold front dropped 108 millimetres of rain on Funchal in five hours, more than the whole of February usually receives, and the water came off the mountains carrying rock and soil down the ribeiras that run through the middle of the city ( 2010 Madeira floods and mudslides ). Mud and boulders swept through streets in Funchal, Ribeira Brava and São Vicente, and the count reached 45 dead with six never found, some 250 injured and around 600 people homeless, at a reconstruction cost near 1.4 billion euros. Anniversary masses are still said, the rebuilt riverbeds through the city are visibly engineered now, and a decade later a handful of families were still without permanent homes. In August 2016 fire came the other way, burning down onto Funchal from the hills and killing three people in their houses.
Tracing a Family in Madeira
Madeira's records are Portuguese, old and unusually well digitised, and its emigration reached further than most.
Parish registers begin in the sixteenth century, the islands having been settled from the 1420s, and the regional archive at Funchal has published a large part of them online free along with the civil registers, the notarial books and passport records.
Sugar built the first Madeiran economy and it was worked by enslaved Africans and Guanches taken from the Canaries, which is a part of the island's history that the records document and the tourism does not mention. Wine built the second, and the British merchant houses that traded it, the Blandys and Leacocks among them, left business archives that record a working population.
Emigration went in several directions at once. Madeirans went to Brazil and Venezuela in large numbers, to South Africa, and to Hawai'i as contract labourers for the sugar plantations from 1878. The most distinctive movement is the Presbyterian one: several thousand Madeirans converted under the preaching of Robert Reid Kalley in the 1840s, were persecuted, and left for Trinidad and then Illinois, founding a Portuguese-speaking Protestant community in Jacksonville that kept its own records.
The other Madeiran export was labour to British Guiana, where Madeirans arrived as indentured workers from 1835 and became a distinct community.
The levadas, the irrigation channels cut across the island by hand, were built largely by that same labour.
Heritage Preservation Efforts
The Laurisilva of Madeira was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1999 as the largest surviving area of laurel forest on earth, believed to be about ninety per cent primary and holding endemic plants and animals including the Madeiran wood pigeon. It's managed by the regional forestry and nature institute, which also maintains the levada walking network as a set of waymarked routes, so protection of the forest and public access to it run through the same office ( IFCN ).
Wine and embroidery are protected by certification rather than by fencing. The old Madeira Wine Institute and the embroidery and handicrafts institute, IBTAM, were merged in 2006 into a single body, IVBAM, which now certifies both: a piece of genuine Madeira embroidery carries a numbered hologram seal, and the wine's designation, its ageing categories and its vintage declarations are policed by the same institute. The result is that a buyer can tell a hand-worked island piece from a machine import, which is the whole point in a trade that was nearly killed by cheap copies.
Funchal's museums cover the layers of the island's economy directly. The Museu A Cidade do Açúcar sits on the excavated site of a fifteenth-century merchant house and tells the sugar story, including the enslaved labour behind it. Quinta das Cruzes occupies the estate associated with Zarco and holds furniture, silver and Manueline stonework. The Casa-Museu Frederico de Freitas preserves a lawyer's collector house with its tiles, prints and watercolours as he left it, and the Museu Etnográfico at Ribeira Brava keeps the tools of levada maintenance, wine pressing, weaving and fishing.
Digital Heritage
The Arquivo e Biblioteca da Madeira holds the archipelago's parish registers, notarial deeds, passport applications and administrative records, and has digitised a large part of them for consultation online. For descendants of Madeiran emigrants, that catalogue is the practical route back: baptism entries from Câmara de Lobos, the passport files that record when a man left for Demerara or Durban, and the notarial papers that show what a family owned before it sold up ( research guide ).
Alongside the archive, the regional culture directorate publishes collection records for the Funchal museums, the wine houses maintain public registers of vintage stock going back well into the nineteenth century, and the levada network is now mapped in detail as GPS trail data, so a route cut by forced labour in the sixteenth century can be downloaded to a phone. The picture is uneven, with the archive well ahead of the museums, but the documentary spine of the island's memory is largely searchable from anywhere.
Contemporary Cultural Influences
Madeirans left in very large numbers, and the destinations were their own: Demerara and the Caribbean in the nineteenth century, then Brazil , then South Africa , Venezuela and the Channel Islands in the twentieth, with Madeiran-descended communities now numbered in the hundreds of thousands in South Africa and around 200,000 in Venezuela ( Madeira ). Jersey's hotel and farming economy has run on Madeiran labour since the 1970s, and Portuguese is widely spoken there. The flow reversed when Venezuela's economy failed: returning families, the venezolanos, brought money, Spanish, arepas and a different idea of city life back into Funchal, and their bakeries and cafés changed the sound of the streets.
At home, the island trades on wine, walking and the poet-and-footballer end of celebrity. Madeira wine has recovered its reputation among serious drinkers, partly because bottles from the nineteenth century remain drinkable and are still poured ( Britannica ). The levada routes have made the island one of the better-known walking destinations in Europe. The poet Herberto Helder was born in Funchal, and Cristiano Ronaldo grew up there, which is why the airport now carries his name.
Success Stories
A working aqueduct became a walking country. The levadas still deliver irrigation water and hydroelectric flow, and their maintenance paths have been waymarked into a trail network that carries the island's tourism without new roads being cut.
The laurel forest survived. Madeira's laurisilva, the largest remaining stand of a forest type that once covered southern Europe, was protected and inscribed by UNESCO in 1999 with most of it still primary growth.
Embroidery kept its name. After a century of cheap imitation, island embroidery is certified piece by piece with a numbered hologram, which preserved both the price and the home-working tradition behind it.
Wine that outlives its makers. Madeira's heated ageing produces a wine effectively immune to oxidation, and bottles laid down two centuries ago are still opened, tasted and sold.
The archive went online. Parish registers, notarial deeds and emigration papers digitised by the Arquivo e Biblioteca da Madeira let descendants in Caracas, Jersey and Johannesburg find the households their families left.

Benedita Câmara
2006 BHC Meeting
Quality is one element of non-price competition, and the decision to produce quality items has various implications according to whether the means adopted is by incorporating added value or by introducing technology. Since quality control was not treated distinctly from the process of production, the case of embroidery on the Portuguese island of Madeira between 1850 and the 1950s is of particular interest as it presents stability of process of production (via the putting out system), yet was subject to different quality control mechanisms. Particular emphasis is given to a situation of market failure (1924-1934) which revealed itself in the form of a drop in the price, demand and reputation of the product and which led to governmental regulation of the quality of the output (1935). The traditional difficulty faced by governments of enforcing regulations was resolved by the intermediary action of an institution (a guild). Acceptance of the quality regulation came from the efficient firms which, since they sold speciality goods, were prepared to share the collective reputation based on aggregate quality. Severe informational asymmetries between consumers and producers explain the idea behind the creation of a regional brand. The sector of production and the functioning of the business structure as an industrial district, in the Marshall sense, explain the level of conflict and bargaining that arose between the firms and the Government which forced them to make investments. By doing so, the traditional form of organization of production was altered. About Madeira's Embroidery
With a centuries-old history, Madeira Embroidery is synonymous with excellence, tradition, detail, art and identity. It is one of the unquestionable brand images of Madeira, the origin of which dates back to the beginning of its colonisation process in the 15th century.
Madeira Embroidery
It is thought that, initially, these embroideries began by being made by noblewomen. Their main purpose was the decoration of household items, clothing and churches. From the beginning, they have been characterised by naturalistic motifs, largely inspired by the island's scenery.
Despite its antiquity, it was only after the second half of the 19th century that Madeira Embroidery was acknowledged at the regional and national level but also internationally since some English traders established in Funchal started exporting this craft work to the United Kingdom.
For this reason, the Madeira Embroidery business gained a more intense momentum. Several houses dedicated to this art appeared and it started to deserve the growing interest of foreigners, such as the British, German or Americans. With that investment, this productive sector suffered a radical improvement, but without ever losing its authenticity.
This work is done manually, with impressive skill and detail. This art has been passed down from generation to generation without ever being lost. Madeira Embroidery is made using materials of excellent quality and smoothness: linen, natural silk, organdie and cotton. This is still the case today, as the authenticity of each piece is carefully certified with the guarantee seal of the Madeira Wine, Embroidery and Handicrafts Institute.
Currently, there are several types of laceworks used for this embroidery, such as Richelieu, 'Caseado' (buttonhole), 'Arrendado' (lace stitch), 'Ponto de Corda' (rope stitch), French stitch or 'Garanito', among others. Given its level of detail, each piece may take several months to be embroidered, depending on the size of the printed design.
The range of products currently produced is also quite diverse. Among others, Madeira Embroidery is used on tables, beds, baths or clothing pieces, for both adults and children. This form of art was recently presented at major international events such as Homo Faber - the largest craft fair in Europe, Paris Fashion Week, thanks to Chanel, and also in the collections by American designer Jeff Garner. It is estimated that there are about one dozen companies currently producing Madeira Embroidery and around 1000 embroiderers who dedicate themselves to the art of embroidery on a daily basis.
