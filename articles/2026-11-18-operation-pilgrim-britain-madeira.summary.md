# Operation Pilgrim and Britain's Secret Plans for Madeira

For nearly three years in World War II, Britain drew up detailed plans to **invade Madeira**, a Portuguese island it had no quarrel with. The island never came under attack. But London worked out, on paper, exactly how troops would land, while Lisbon quietly built guns meant to stop them.

Madeira's story runs through **two separate British operations**, three years apart, and a Portuguese defense plan built in between. Here is how it unfolded, year by year.

## Portugal Declares Neutrality on 1 September 1939

Portugal declared itself **neutral** on 1 September 1939, the day Germany invaded Poland. The 600-year-old Anglo-Portuguese Alliance stayed in place. Britain did not ask for Portuguese help, so Lisbon stayed out of the fighting.

A British memorandum, sent four days later on 5 September, confirmed that understanding in writing. Portugal's overseas territories, including Madeira, had **almost no capacity** to defend themselves against a real attack.

## France's Collapse in June 1940 Puts Madeira's Defenses in Motion

France surrendered to Germany in **June 1940**. German tanks reached the Pyrenees. Some of General Francisco Franco's ministers in Spain floated the idea of entering the war and marching into Portugal.

Lisbon responded on two fronts. In July, it reinforced its 1939 non-aggression pact with Madrid, to keep the whole Iberian Peninsula out of the fighting. At the same time, Portugal's government approved a **defense plan** for Madeira.

The plan named **no specific enemy**. Anyone threatening Portuguese sovereignty, Axis or Allied, counted as hostile.

Colonel Luiz Augusto de Sousa Rodrigues, one of the plan's authors, judged an airborne attack on the island **next to impossible**. The island's distance from Europe and its steep terrain ruled it out, in his view. The real danger was a landing from the sea.

The plan made defending **Funchal**, the capital, the top priority, and leaned on local manpower. Engineers sketched a chain of small forts along the coastline, built into natural caves where the ground allowed. Their job was to stop an amphibious landing before it reached the beach.

## Three Krupp Guns Reach Pico Da Cruz in 139 Days

Memories of an earlier war still weighed on Funchal. German submarines had **shelled the harbor** twice, in 1916 and 1917, and islanders had no wish to see it happen again.

Work began on Madeira's first coastal battery on **5 July 1940**, at Pico da Cruz in São Martinho. The hilltop overlooks both Praia Formosa beach and the bay of Funchal. The guns themselves did not arrive until 25 July, aboard the steamer Carvalho Araújo, and sat at the docks for days, held up by customs.

Moving the guns up the hill took weeks. One barrel alone needed **two days** to cover roughly 200 meters. Crews hauled it by crane and winch over ground so steep that the gun pedestals kept sinking into the soil.

The battery was finished on **21 November 1940**, 139 days after work began. Builders had moved 7,000 cubic meters of earth and poured 2,000 cubic meters of concrete. In the end, 130 laborers mounted three Krupp 15cm guns with a range of 14 kilometers.

## Three British Plans Merge Into Operation Pilgrim by March 1941

While Madeira's gunners worked, London was drawing up contingency plans of its own. On **23 October 1940**, Adolf Hitler and General Franco met at Hendaye, on the French-Spanish border, to discuss Spain joining the war. The meeting ended without agreement.

Britain still feared Franco might side with Germany and **let it seize** Gibraltar. Germany had the same islands in mind.

Führer Directive No. 18, issued on 12 November 1940, set out **Operation Felix**, a plan to attack Gibraltar. It required occupying Portugal's Azores first.

Britain answered with **three plans** of its own. Operation Thruster covered the Azores, Operation Springboard covered Madeira, and Operation Puma covered the Canaries and Cape Verde.

On 12 March 1941, Britain's Joint Planning Staff combined all three into one document. The new scheme took the name **Operation Pilgrim**, and kept the Canaries as its main target.

## Pilgrim's September 1941 Order Targets Gran Canaria Instead

Prime Minister **Winston Churchill** set out the stakes that spring, in a government memo:

> If we are forced from Gibraltar, we must take the Canaries immediately, allowing us to control the western entry to the Mediterranean.

**Winston Churchill**, Prime Minister of the United Kingdom

Planners eventually **dropped Madeira** from the active plan. They had learned that Portugal's government meant to retreat to the Azores if the mainland were invaded.

From there, Lisbon would ask Britain or the United States for protection. That news let them **simplify Pilgrim's scope**.

The final operational order, signed on 20 September 1941, sent Major General Victor Odlum's Canadian 2nd Division, about **24,000 men**, to capture Gran Canaria instead. Their targets were the harbor at La Luz and the airfield at Gando.

One battleship, four aircraft carriers, three cruisers and 27 destroyers were assigned to escort the landing. A 30-strong sabotage squadron stood ready to **parachute onto Tenerife**. The plan stayed on standby for the rest of 1941, then was formally cancelled in February 1942, once Spain declared itself fully neutral.

## Operation Ripper Plans a Landing at Praia Formosa in 1942

Madeira's reprieve did not last. Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union, and the United States' entry into the war, renewed Britain's worries about the Atlantic. By February 1942, London had drawn up a new plan aimed only at Madeira, under its own code name: **Operation Ripper**.

A first draft, circulated on **26 February 1942** to the Admiralty, the Air Ministry and Britain's logistics commands, set the goal as occupying the island. The point was to resupply destroyers and cruisers on patrol, with the bay of Funchal available as a possible seaplane base. The outline was fixed by March, and by April the whole plan was ready for approval that summer, if needed.

Commandos would land in darkness at **Praia Formosa**, the very beach Madeira's gunners at Pico da Cruz had been built to cover. British planners judged the island's own defenses "obsolete". They noted the lack of any air defense and rated the available equipment scarce.

Under normal conditions, planners expected the whole operation to take **seven days**. It involved 4,200 men and 160 vehicles.

British planners also weighed the people they would meet on shore. They counted on an influential **British community** on the island, and on many Madeirans known to favor the Allies. Planners separately noted several identified pro-Nazi residents.

## Portugal Builds Up Madeira's Air Defenses Through 1943

Lisbon kept reinforcing Madeira while Ripper sat ready in London. The island's first anti-aircraft battery landed on **10 January 1942**, aboard the ship Lima. It brought 114 men from the Anti-Aircraft Artillery Group in Cascais, mainland Portugal's pioneering air-defense unit.

By 1943, Madeira's air defenses were complete. Fixed guns stood at **São Martinho** and at Palheiro Ferreiro, with mobile Bofors pieces scattered around the city and a mobile searchlight battery. The island's British-made Vickers 9.4cm guns were officially anti-aircraft weapons, but could also fire on ships, backing up the coastal battery at Pico da Cruz.

## The August 1943 Azores Deal Ends the Threat to Madeira

Operation Ripper's moment never came. Allied landings in North Africa that November, under **Operation Torch**, gradually pulled British attention away from the Atlantic islands.

In May 1943, at the **Trident Conference** in Washington, Churchill and Roosevelt briefly looked at Ripper again. They worried about a renewed German submarine campaign.

Three developments then made the operation unnecessary. The German navy withdrew its U-boats, the Allies succeeded in Italy, and Britain struck a **new deal** with Lisbon.

On **17 August 1943**, Britain and Portugal signed an agreement in Lisbon, invoking the Anglo-Portuguese Alliance. It let British forces use the ports of Horta and Ponta Delgada, and the airfield at Lajes, in the Azores from that October.

With the Azores secured through **diplomacy**, not force, Britain no longer needed Madeira, the Canaries or Cape Verde as a substitute base. Operation Ripper was quietly shelved. Operation Pilgrim had already been cancelled the year before.

A historian who studied Madeira's wartime defenses in the island's own military archives, Fábio David Rodrigues, reached a clear verdict. Its batteries and anti-aircraft guns would likely have been **too weak** to stop a determined Allied landing. Against Germany, which had no aircraft carrier free for such an assault, they might have put up real resistance.
