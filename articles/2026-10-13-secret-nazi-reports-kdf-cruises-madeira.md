# Gestapo Agents Sailed Undercover on Madeira's 1930s Nazi Cruises

Agents of the Gestapo, the Nazi secret state police, travelled **in disguise** on the ships that brought German tourists to Funchal in the 1930s. They wrote secret reports that described Portugal in far harsher terms than anything printed at the time. The reports are set out in a study of the Atlantic cruises by Mário Matos, of the University of Minho.

## About Ten Atlantic Cruises Brought 20,000 German Tourists to Portugal

The cruises were run by Kraft durch Freude (KdF), or Strength Through Joy, the leisure organisation of the Nazi regime. Between 1935 and 1939 it organised **about ten cruises** to Lisbon and Madeira, which carried roughly 20,000 German tourists to Portuguese territory. A few hundred Austrians joined after Germany annexed Austria in 1938.

Each trip lasted about two weeks, with stops of **two to three days** in Lisbon and Funchal. The cruises rarely used a single ship. A fleet of three or more liners usually sailed together, carrying around three thousand tourists at a time. The Azores were on the route only once, in the spring of 1935, because their climate suited visitors looking for sun less well.

## Fewer Than 20 Percent of the Passengers Were Really Workers

The regime sold these voyages as Arbeiterkreuzfahrten, or **workers' cruises**. Matos writes that not even 20 percent of the tourists who took them belonged to the working class. On average, not even one German in a thousand was able to go.

KdF was founded in 1933 inside the German Labour Front, the Deutsche Arbeitsfront (DAF), led by **[Robert Ley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Ley)**. The regime had dismantled the trade unions on 2 May 1933 and replaced them with the DAF, which every worker had to join. Tourism researchers quoted in the study call KdF "the largest tour operator of its time worldwide".

:::callout 💡
**In short**: the trips were advertised as workers' cruises, but fewer than one in five passengers came from the working class.
:::

## Gestapo Men of Trust Reported on Poverty and Child Begging

The published account of these voyages was warm. The secret one was not. Gestapo agents known as **Men of Trust** for Foreign Travel took part in the cruises in disguise and kept a strange kind of travel diary. They watched the German tourists as closely as they watched the countries they visited.

One report reads: "The decay and the dirt of Lisbon and of the great mass of its inhabitants is so striking that our tourists returned on board deeply shaken." Another turns to the **children in the streets**: "What becomes especially unbearable for a foreigner is the child begging."

The agents cared most about what the comparison did for the regime at home. Nearly every secret report Matos examined calls the foreign trips useful for keeping **social peace** in Germany. One concluded that a comparison of the two countries "came out unequivocally in favour of Germany".

:::callout 💡
**What is missing**: the study does not say how many Men of Trust sailed on each cruise, how many of their reports survive, or whether the Portuguese authorities knew that Gestapo agents were on board.
:::

## Robert Ley Said in 1938 the Trips Would Fortify German Nerves

Ley was blunt about the purpose when he spoke to his own people. Aboard a KdF ship in **1938**, he said the aim was not to "create an association of excursionists, nor a club of amusement" for "merely getting to know Italy or seeing Portugal".

The point, he said, was to "fortify the nerves of the Germans". The "80 million Germans" should then be able to face the "most urgent question of the lack of soil" with vigour, once the Führer decided to solve it. Matos reads the cruises as one part of the preparation for the **conquest of living space**.

## Travel Books Published in 1936 Called Madeirans Small and Quiet

The writers the regime took on board left a record of their own. **Four titles** carry the date 1936:

- 'Volk zu Schiff' by Jakob Schaffner
- 'Der Sonne entgegen' by Hans Biallas
- the anthology 'KdF - Das grosse Urlauberschiff', edited by Otto Paust
- 'Petermann fährt nach Madeira', a comedy by August Hinrichs

Schaffner called Madeira "a purely natural product" and its people "human beings who live like the animals or the flowers". He set the "quiet, small and friendly southern islanders" against the KdF tourists, whom he called "the tall, blond and active Nibelungs". The islanders, he wrote, did not **seek freedom or power** as the Germans did.

## Salazar's Government Set Up Its Own Leisure Body in May 1935

In Portugal the visits were welcomed. The press was censored, and Matos describes its coverage as broadly **approving**. On 17 March 1935, O Século praised the visitors' "order, discipline and joy". Three days later O Comércio do Porto wrote that "it is flattering for us to record that Portugal is the first country to receive this embassy of peace and work".

Officials went further than the newspapers. Writing in the Diário da Manhã of 8 October 1937, **António de Menezes** of the National Propaganda Secretariat called KdF "our best propaganda agent" in Germany. Ley himself was received by President **[Óscar Carmona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%93scar_Carmona)** during the first cruise, in March 1935.

Two months after that cruise, in **May 1935**, Portugal set up its own leisure organisation, the Fundação Nacional para a Alegria no Trabalho, or National Foundation for Joy at Work. Matos cites a study by José Carlos Valente showing it was modelled directly on the German body. The cruises stopped in 1939, and **[António de Oliveira Salazar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3nio_de_Oliveira_Salazar)** chose neutrality as the war began.
